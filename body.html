The Rebuild

Build a body capable of carrying your life

Real lived experience. Practical reflection. One place to begin.

Back to the Circle ↗

When I was younger, training was probably more about appearance than anything else.

I wanted to look better.

I was always on the smaller side, so getting bigger felt like a way to feel more confident and prove something.

There was definitely some showing off mixed into it.

And I don’t think there’s anything wrong with wanting to look good.

But over time, the reason I train has changed.

Now I care a lot more about being healthy, capable, energetic, and present for the people who depend on me.

I still like looking good.

I still want to take pride in how I take care of myself.

But that isn’t the whole point anymore.

Fitness started affecting more than my body

One thing my wife has pointed out to me is that when I stop training consistently, I’m different.

My patience gets shorter.

I can get more irritable.

I’m not quite myself.

And when I’m training regularly, I feel better mentally too.

I’m more confident.

More disciplined.

More patient.

More like the version of myself I actually want to be.

That taught me something:

Taking care of your body doesn’t stay in the gym.

It follows you home.

I want energy left for my kids

One of the things I appreciate most now is having enough energy to actually play with my kids.

When families are together, it feels good being the dad who still has enough in the tank to run around, play, wrestle, and be involved instead of always being too tired.

Not because I’m trying to prove I’m better than another dad.

Because those years are short.

My kids aren’t going to stay little forever.

And one day, I want them to remember a dad who participated.

Not just a dad who worked hard.

Not just a dad who paid the bills.

A dad who got down on the floor and played.

That has become a much bigger motivation than simply looking good.

I think men can fall into two extremes

There’s one side of fitness culture that can make it seem like your worth as a man is tied to how you look.

Big enough.

Lean enough.

Strong enough.

And if you don’t look a certain way, somehow you’re less of a man.

I don’t believe that.

But I also don’t think the answer is giving up on your physical health completely.

There’s another extreme where a man stops taking care of himself and then convinces himself it doesn’t matter.

Stops moving.

Stops putting effort into his health.

Stops caring how he feels.

And sometimes starts resenting people who are doing the work he knows he probably should be doing too.

I don’t think either extreme is healthy.

The goal isn’t obsession.

And it isn’t neglect.

The goal is stewardship.

Take care of the body you have so it can serve the life you’re trying to build.

Strength should serve something

To me, being physically strong means more than moving weight in a gym.

It means being capable.

Having enough fitness to work hard.

Carry responsibilities.

Keep up with your family.

Handle stressful days.

Serve other people when needed.

And still have something left afterward.

I work a physically demanding job.

I’m involved in firefighting.

I have a family.

So I don’t want fitness that only looks good in a mirror.

I want a body that works.

Strength.

Mobility.

Endurance.

Energy.

Recovery.

All of it matters.

The goal isn’t obsession. And it isn’t neglect. The goal is stewardship.

Doing hard things changes more than your muscles

Some days I don’t want to train.

I’m tired.

Work was long.

There are other things I would rather do.

But there is something important about following through anyway.

Every time you do something difficult that you told yourself you were going to do, you build something mentally too.

Discipline.

Confidence.

Trust in yourself.

You start proving:

“I can do things even when I don’t feel like doing them.”

And that carries into other areas of life.

You become a little harder to knock off course.

Not because you never struggle.

Because you’ve practiced showing up anyway.

You don’t need the perfect program

I think one of the biggest mistakes men make is believing it only counts if they can do everything perfectly.

Perfect workout.

Perfect meal plan.

Perfect schedule.

And when life interrupts that plan, they stop completely.

But something is almost always better than nothing.

Maybe today you have time for an hour.

Maybe tomorrow you only have twenty minutes.

Maybe you need to walk.

Stretch.

Lift.

Recover.

Go to bed earlier.

The exact move matters less than continuing to take responsibility for yourself.

Consistency beats constantly starting over.

“I’m too tired.”

I understand this one.

I work a full-time physical job.

There are days when I’ve already done a ton of stairs and physical work before I ever get home.

Sometimes there are other responsibilities waiting afterward.

And then I walk through the door and my kids want me.

So yes — sometimes you’re legitimately exhausted.

And rest matters.

But I think you still have to ask yourself a bigger question:

What kind of life am I building over the next ten or fifteen years?

Because the easy choice today is usually obvious.

Get home.

Do the minimum.

Sit down.

Zone out.

Tell yourself you earned it.

And sometimes you probably did.

But if that becomes the pattern every night, years disappear quickly.

The alternative is harder.

Take care of yourself.

Build your fitness.

Build your energy.

Keep moving even when motivation isn’t there.

At first, it can suck.

But over time, your capacity grows.

And suddenly you have more energy than you used to.

More energy for your spouse.

More energy for your kids.

More energy for the things you say matter most.

Think about what you want your kids to remember

Ten or fifteen years from now, I probably won’t remember most of the nights I could have spent zoning out after work.

But my kids may remember whether I played with them.

Whether I showed up.

Whether I had energy for them.

Whether I was involved in their life.

That perspective changes things for me.

I don’t want to look back and realize that I constantly chose what was easiest in the moment and missed what mattered in the long run.

Sometimes the harder choice today creates the memories you’re grateful for later.

A simple place to begin

Don’t try to transform everything tomorrow.

Choose one physical promise you can actually keep this week.

Train three times.

Walk every day.

Go to bed earlier.

Drink more water.

Do ten minutes of mobility.

Whatever it is, make it realistic.

Then keep your word.

After that, build another layer.

The goal isn’t to destroy yourself.

The goal is to become consistent.

What rebuilding your body means to me

Your body doesn’t determine your value as a man.

You don’t need a certain look to earn respect or worth.

But your health affects how you experience your life.

Your energy.

Your mood.

Your confidence.

Your ability to work.

Your ability to serve.

Your ability to be present with the people you love.

So take care of yourself.

Not because you hate your body.

Not because you need to prove anything.

Because your life asks something from you.

Build a body capable of carrying it.

And when your kids ask you to play, try to have something left in the tank.

Keep going
