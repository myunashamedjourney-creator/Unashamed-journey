The Rebuild

Where the work becomes visible

Real lived experience. Practical reflection. One place to begin.

Back to the Circle ↗

Growing up without a healthy father, I had an idea in my head of what a dad was supposed to be.

Someone who stayed.

Someone who protected you.

Someone who wanted the best for you.

Someone you could trust.

So when I eventually had a father figure in my life, I thought maybe I was finally going to experience that.

Instead, that relationship became abusive.

And it completely disrupted the picture I had built in my head of what a father was supposed to be.

The person who was supposed to make me feel safer became somebody I was afraid of.

That changes the way you approach relationships.

I learned to protect myself instead of letting people know me

A lot of the patterns I’ve talked about in the Mind and Identity sections showed up in my relationships too.

People-pleasing.

Changing who I was depending on who I was around.

Keeping parts of myself hidden.

Always being somewhat prepared for somebody to leave.

I wanted people close to me.

But I was also afraid of what could happen if I actually let them get close.

So people often knew a version of me.

Not necessarily all of me.

And when you spend enough time doing that, genuine relationships become difficult.

Because closeness requires something the mask can’t give you:

honesty.

You eventually have to let somebody see the parts of you that aren’t polished.

The fears.

The insecurity.

The mistakes.

The things you’re still working through.

Otherwise people can love the person you’re presenting while you still feel completely unknown.

Presence over provision

This has become one of the most important ideas behind Unashamed Journey.

Presence over provision.

And this one is personal for me.

I grew up with very little.

I know what it feels like when money is tight and your family doesn’t have the things other families seem to have.

So naturally, when I became a father, part of me wanted to make sure my kids never experienced that.

I want to provide.

I want them to have opportunities.

I want to give them a good life.

There’s nothing wrong with that.

The problem is that provision can become an incredibly easy way to justify absence.

You tell yourself:

“I’m doing all of this for them.”

And technically, you might be.

But if constantly providing for your family means you’re never actually with your family, eventually you have to ask whether the balance has shifted too far.

Good things can still steal your attention

Work isn’t the only thing that can do this.

Even Unashamed Journey can.

I believe in what I’m building.

I want to help men.

I want this brand to become something meaningful.

But there are times when I can get locked into making content because I haven’t posted yet or because I want the next video to be better.

And I have to remind myself:

The mission cannot become an excuse to ignore the people the mission is supposed to help me become better for.

Sometimes posting the good-enough video and spending that extra time with my kids is the better decision.

Because content can be made tomorrow.

That version of my kids won’t exist forever.

You don’t get this version of them back

I’ve started realizing this more as my kids grow.

They change incredibly fast.

And I try to remind myself to appreciate who they are right now.

Not who they’ll become.

Not what life will look like next year.

Right now.

Because one day you’re holding a little kid who wants you involved in everything.

And before you know it, that version of them is gone.

You don’t get it back.

That changes the question from:

“Do I have time to play right now?”

to:

“How many more times will they ask me like this?”

That perspective makes presence feel different.

Providing still matters

Presence over provision doesn’t mean provision doesn’t matter.

It does.

Your family needs stability.

Bills have to be paid.

Responsibilities matter.

Working hard is a good thing.

The mistake is thinking financial provision is the complete definition of being a good husband or father.

Your family also needs your attention.

Your patience.

Your affection.

Your consistency.

Your ability to listen.

Your willingness to repair when you mess up.

Sometimes the thing your family needs most from you cannot be bought.

THE REBUILD

Relationships are where all the other work becomes visible.

Leadership isn’t control

When I talk about men leading their families, I’m not talking about being a dictator.

Leadership isn’t:

“Everybody does what I say because I’m the man.”

To me, leadership is responsibility.

Service.

Sacrifice.

Going first.

Being somebody your family can depend on when things get difficult.

Being willing to admit when you’re wrong.

Keeping yourself steady enough that somebody else having a bad day doesn’t automatically destroy yours.

And trying to create an environment where the people you love feel safe, supported, and known.

This is where the whole Rebuild Circle comes together

The more I think about it, the more Relationships feels like the place where the other four areas become visible.

If my Faith is grounded, I have something deeper than myself to build my family on.

If I’m taking care of my Mind, I’m better able to recognize my triggers, regulate my emotions, and not dump everything happening inside me onto the people closest to me.

If I’m taking care of my Body, I have more energy to work, serve, play, and actually participate in family life.

If my Identity is secure, somebody else’s bad mood, criticism, or rejection doesn’t have to completely determine who I am.

And then all of that reaches outward into my Relationships.

That’s why these five areas are connected.

You don’t rebuild one part of yourself in isolation.

The work eventually touches everything.

Your family experiences the version of you that comes home

It’s possible to be respected at work and difficult to live with.

To be dependable for everybody else and unavailable to your own family.

To give strangers your patience all day and come home with none left.

That’s something I have to pay attention to myself.

Because the people closest to us often get whatever energy is left.

But those are usually the relationships we claim matter most.

I don’t want my family constantly receiving the leftovers.

Repair matters more than pretending you never mess up

I’m not going to get every interaction right.

Neither are you.

There will be moments when you lose patience.

Say something badly.

Miss what somebody needed.

Get defensive.

Become distracted.

Being a good husband, father, or friend doesn’t mean never getting it wrong.

It means becoming willing to repair it.

Own your part.

Apologize without explaining away the apology.

Listen.

Ask what you could have done differently.

Then try to do better next time.

Your family doesn’t need perfection.

They need to know that when something breaks between you, you care enough to repair it.

If you’re working your ass off and they still feel distant

If a man told me:

“Everything I do is for my family. I work constantly. I provide for them. But my wife says I’m distant and my kids always want more of me. What else am I supposed to do?”

I’d tell him:

I believe you when you say you’re doing it for them.

But maybe they’re telling you what they actually need.

And it might not be more money.

Maybe they need more of you.

Your attention.

Your time.

Your laughter.

Your patience.

Your presence.

Providing for somebody is valuable.

But being present with them is something money can’t replace.

A simple place to begin

Tonight, ask yourself:

Who has been getting the best version of me lately?

My work?

My phone?

My hobbies?

My goals?

Or the people I say matter most?

Then do one thing that makes somebody you love feel seen.

Put your phone away.

Play.

Listen.

Handle something around the house without being asked.

Have the conversation you’ve been avoiding.

Tell somebody what they mean to you.

It doesn’t have to be dramatic.

Presence is usually built in ordinary moments.

What rebuilding relationships means to me

I grew up wanting a father who would stay, protect me, and genuinely want the best for me.

I cannot change the fact that I didn’t always receive that.

But I can decide what the people in my life receive from me.

I can be present.

I can keep learning.

I can apologize.

I can listen.

I can provide without allowing provision to replace connection.

I can become a safer, steadier, more dependable man than some of the examples I was given.

And maybe that’s one of the most powerful things we can do with the pain we inherited:

Stop passing it forward.

Your family doesn’t need a perfect man.

They need a man willing to keep doing the work.

Be present.

Go first.

Become the man you needed.

Keep going
