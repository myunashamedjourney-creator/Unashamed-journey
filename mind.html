The Rebuild

When survival patterns outlive the danger

Real lived experience. Practical reflection. One place to begin.

Back to the Circle ↗

Growing up, I learned how to adapt.

I got very good at reading a room.

Changing who I was depending on who I was around.

Keeping parts of myself hidden.

Looking back, I almost developed a chameleon mentality.

When life felt chaotic, I learned to go inward. Put on the mask. Become whoever I needed to be in that moment.

And after losing my mom, something in me shut down even more.

I think part of me decided:

If I don’t care too deeply, losing something can’t hurt me as badly.

If somebody leaves, fine.

If something gets taken away, fine.

If I never really put emotional skin in the game, maybe nothing can break me again.

At the time, that protected me.

Eventually, it started costing me.

I learned how to hide my own story

As I got older, I didn’t want people knowing where I came from.

I didn’t want to be the orphan.

I didn’t want people knowing about the abuse, instability, or things I had lived through.

So sometimes I would rewrite my own story.

Leave things out.

Make things up.

Become a version of myself that felt easier to show people.

And the longer I did that, the harder it became to let anybody actually know me.

People could know the version of me I created.

They couldn’t always know me.

That mask gave me control over what people could see.

But it also made genuine connection almost impossible.

You can’t leave me if I’ve already left you

Another pattern followed me into adulthood.

I expected people to leave.

I expected people to hurt me.

I expected relationships to eventually go bad.

So I stayed ready.

If someone started questioning me, getting frustrated with me, or getting too close to something I didn’t want exposed, my instinct was to pull away.

Sometimes completely.

I could ghost somebody and move on.

Because somewhere underneath that behaviour was this belief:

You can’t abandon me if I leave first.

That can feel like strength.

It isn’t.

It’s protection.

And protection has a cost.

I lost relationships because of it.

I lost a close friendship because I never truly let that person in. I wasn’t fully honest about who I was or what I carried.

And eventually that relationship disappeared.

There are some doors you can close so quickly that you don’t realize until later how much you wanted what was on the other side.

Your mind can keep fighting battles that aren’t happening anymore

This is one of the hardest things I’ve had to learn.

Sometimes life changes before your internal reactions do.

You can finally be surrounded by people who love you.

Build a family.

Create stability.

Become somebody you’re proud of.

And part of your mind can still be standing guard like everything is about to disappear.

Expecting betrayal.

Expecting rejection.

Looking for the first sign somebody is angry.

Preparing to defend yourself before there is anything to defend against.

That doesn’t mean you’re doomed to stay there.

But you have to become aware of it.

Because a pattern you can’t see gets to make decisions for you.

Parenting has exposed parts of me I still need to work on

One of the clearest places I see this now is as a father.

I care deeply about raising my kids well.

I want them to understand boundaries.

Respect authority.

Become strong, capable people who can lead themselves.

But sometimes one of my kids pushes a boundary, argues, disobeys, or hits exactly the wrong button when I’m already frustrated.

And sometimes I snap.

Those moments bother me.

Because one of my deepest fears is becoming like the kind of father figure who once terrified me.

I don’t want my children growing up experiencing the things I experienced.

So when I see myself reacting in ways I don’t like, it forces me to look inward.

Sometimes my wife will remind me that some of the traits that frustrate me in my son are traits that look a lot like me.

And I’ve had to wonder:

Am I reacting to something wrong in him — or am I reacting to a part of myself that I was taught wasn’t acceptable?

That’s uncomfortable.

But that’s the work.

THE REBUILD

The mask can keep pain out — but it can keep love out too.

Self-awareness doesn’t mean hating yourself

For a long time, I think I misunderstood growth.

I thought seeing your flaws meant beating yourself up over them.

Now I see it differently.

Self-awareness isn’t:

“Look how messed up I am.”

It’s:

“Okay. That’s what I’m doing. Why am I doing it? And what do I want to do differently next time?”

That change matters.

Because shame usually makes me want to hide.

Awareness gives me something I can actually work with.

I had to rebuild who I was

There was a season of my life where a lot disappeared at once.

Relationships changed.

Friendships disappeared.

My sense of community changed.

My faith was being reconstructed.

And I had to figure out:

Who am I when all the masks and outside validation get stripped away?

That didn’t happen overnight.

It took time.

It took honest conversations.

It took listening when my wife pointed something out that I didn’t necessarily want to hear.

It took learning my own patterns.

It took faith.

It took failing and trying again.

But eventually I started becoming comfortable with the person in the mirror.

I’m not perfect.

There are still things about myself I’m working on.

But I’m no longer trying to completely escape who I am.

And that is a freedom I didn’t have when I was younger.

The mask can keep pain out — but it can keep love out too

This is what I would tell another man who’s always waiting for something bad to happen.

I understand why you built the wall.

Maybe it was necessary once.

Maybe staying guarded protected you.

Maybe not trusting people kept you from getting hurt again.

But if you never lower the wall, you don’t only block pain.

You can block friendship.

Marriage.

Community.

Brotherhood.

Love.

Being truly known.

You can become so good at protecting yourself from losing people that you never fully experience having them.

At some point, survival has to become living.

A simple place to begin

The next time you feel yourself reacting strongly, don’t immediately judge the reaction.

Get curious.

Ask:

What just happened?

What am I feeling underneath my first reaction?

What am I expecting to happen next?

Has this person actually done something wrong, or is my mind preparing for an old outcome?

What would a better response look like right now?

Sometimes that pause is enough to change the direction of the whole conversation.

You might still feel angry.

Still feel afraid.

Still want to shut down.

But now you have a choice.

Let people help you see what you can’t

Some patterns are almost impossible to recognize from inside yourself.

That is why trustworthy people matter.

My wife has helped me see things I couldn’t always see on my own.

That can be uncomfortable.

But if somebody genuinely loves you and repeatedly points out the same pattern, it may be worth listening before immediately defending yourself.

You don’t have to accept every criticism somebody gives you.

But you should be willing to ask:

Is there something here I need to see?

Growth gets harder when nobody is allowed close enough to tell you the truth.

What rebuilding your mind means to me

You can’t change what happened.

You can’t go back and give the younger version of yourself a completely different life.

And I don’t think the goal is to hate the survival mechanisms that got you through it.

Some of them probably helped you survive something difficult.

But you can eventually ask:

Do I still need this?

Do I still need the mask?

Do I still need to leave first?

Do I still need to expect betrayal?

Do I still need to hide every part of myself that feels vulnerable?

Because you may not.

Your past can explain why the pattern exists.

It doesn’t have to decide whether the pattern stays.

The mask protected you.

Now learn when it’s safe to take it off.

Keep going
